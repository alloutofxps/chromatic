Pairing Advisor

Tell it one colour you already have. It might be a shirt you already own, a jacket you are trying to match, or a length of fabric you just bought. It gives you back a list of colours that go with it. Every colour has a name, a reason it works, and an idea of where to put it.

1

Pick your colour first. Use the colour box, type in a hex code, or search 190 colours by name.

2

The results come in five groups. Each group does a different job, and every colour tells you where it belongs.

3

Tap any colour you like. It gets saved to your palette in the bar at the bottom of the screen.

Palette Library

These colour sets work because real people chose them and kept using them. No formula was involved. The Wada sets come from a colour dictionary published by the Japanese painter Sanzo Wada in the 1930s. The reference sets are combinations that keep turning up in painting, fashion and interiors.

1

Use the buttons to filter. You can narrow it down by mood, or by where the set came from.

2

Every set explains itself. It tells you why those colours hold together and what you might use them for.

3

Send one to the Palette Lab. There you can change the amounts and see it on an outfit or a painting.

The colour names in the Wada sets come from an older naming system he worked from. The hex codes here are close modern matches. The originals were printed with ink, so no screen colour can ever be exact.

Wheel & Harmony

Click anywhere on the wheel to pick a colour. Then build one of six classic colour sets from it. This wheel is laid out the way real paint behaves, which is something most colour tools online get wrong.

1

Click the wheel to pick a colour. Everything the app can tell you about it appears next to the wheel.

2

Switch between Paint and Screen. In paint the opposite of red is green. On a screen it is cyan.

3

Leave Refine switched on. It softens the maths into colours you would actually wear.

Why Refine is switched on to begin with. The plain maths gives every colour the same brightness and the same strength. That is exactly why colour sets from generators so often look cheap. Refine changes them the way a designer would, so one colour leads and the others support it. Switch it off if you want to see the raw version.

The same colours used in different amounts give you a completely different result. The old rule of thumb is 60 / 30 / 10. That means one colour leads, a second one supports it, and a third shows up in small doses. In an outfit that is usually a coat, then trousers, then a bag. When a palette does not work, the amounts are usually to blame long before the colours are.

Which colours suit you

    
Personal Colour

    
Just under the surface, your skin has a warm or a cool tint to it. That tint is called your undertone. Colours that match it make your skin look even and your eyes look brighter. Colours that fight it make you look tired. You can measure yours with the camera, or answer six questions instead.

  


  

    
    
  


  
  

    

      
1
Stand facing a window. Daylight works best. Take your glasses off, and try not to have make-up on your forehead, cheeks or jaw.

      
2
Line your face up in the oval. Hold still. The app reads four small patches of skin: your forehead, both cheeks and your jaw.

      
3
You get real measurements back. Not a guess. Then a set of colours worked out from your own numbers.

    


    

      🔒
      
Nothing leaves your phone. There is no server here and nothing gets uploaded. The picture is read, measured, and then thrown away. The only thing kept is a short list of numbers, and you can delete those at any time.

One thing to keep in mind. The season system is a styling method that stylists have used for decades. It is not a medical test. The scan does measure your skin honestly, but the light in the room, your camera, the fabric and the cut of a garment all still matter. Plenty of people look wonderful in colours this system says they should avoid. Treat it as a good place to start rather than a rule you have to follow.
